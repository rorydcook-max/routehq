*By Anan · Updated 28 October 2026*

A scooter rental business looks simple from the outside: buy some bikes, put up a sign, hand over keys. The shops that last are the ones that treat it as a small finance business. Each bike is an asset that has to earn back its price, cover its running costs and survive renters who are not always careful.

This guide walks through the decisions in the order you will meet them. The numbers are examples to show the method. Plug in your own local prices before you commit money.

> This guide is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by country and sometimes by province, so confirm the details with your local office.

## Choose your location and your customers

Your customers decide almost everything else: which bikes you buy, what you charge, how you take deposits and where you advertise. There are two main groups, and most shops serve both in different proportions.

**Short-stay tourists** rent for one to seven days. They pay the highest daily rate, book late, find you on Google Maps or walk past your shop, and care about convenience: delivery to the hotel, clean helmets, a quick handover. Demand swings hard between high and low season.

**Long-stay renters** (digital nomads, expats, seasonal workers, students, local people between bikes) rent for one to twelve months. They pay much less per day but fill your bikes for weeks at a time, need fewer handovers and smooth out your low season. They compare prices carefully and stay with shops that fix problems quickly.

When you pick a location, look for:

- **Walk-past traffic or easy delivery.** A shop near guesthouses, a pier or a busy beach road gets walk-ins. If rent there is high, a small yard plus a delivery service can work just as well.
- **Competition you can beat on something.** Count the shops nearby and check their Google reviews. If everyone has old bikes and bad reviews about deposits, newer bikes and a clear deposit policy are your edge.
- **Secure overnight parking.** You need somewhere locked and ideally covered for every bike that is not rented.
- **A mechanic you can reach.** Being near a reliable workshop saves hours of downtime.

## How many bikes to start with, and which models

Start smaller than you think. Five to ten bikes is enough to learn your local demand, your real repair costs and how much of your time the business takes. You can add bikes once you see them staying rented.

Choose models that are easy to rent, easy to repair and easy to resell:

| Type | Typical examples | Good for | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 110–125cc automatic | Honda Click, Scoopy, Beat; Yamaha Fino, Mio | Most tourists, long-stay renters | Cheapest to buy and fix; the backbone of most fleets |
| 150–160cc automatic | Honda ADV 160, PCX 160; Yamaha NMAX, Aerox | Couples, longer rides, hills | Higher daily rate; popular for monthly rentals |
| 250–350cc maxi scooter | Honda Forza, ADV 350; Yamaha XMAX | Experienced riders, touring | Higher deposit and insurance; fewer customers |
| Semi-automatic/manual | Honda Wave, Yamaha Sirius | Local renters, rural areas | Low cost; many tourists cannot ride them |

A practical starting mix is mostly 125cc automatics, plus one or two 150–160cc bikes once you see demand. Stick to one or two brands so spare parts, keys and know-how carry across the fleet. Pick colours and models that are common locally, because they are easier to resell.

## Buying new, used or on finance

**New bikes** cost more up front but come with a warranty, fewer breakdowns and the best photos for your listings. They also hold resale value for the first two or three years.

**Used bikes** can halve your startup cost. The trade-off is that you inherit someone else's maintenance history. Before you buy, check:

- The registration book matches the frame and engine numbers on the bike.
- Road tax and compulsory insurance are current, and there is no outstanding finance on the bike.
- Tyres, brakes, belt and battery condition (budget to replace anything doubtful).
- How it starts cold, idles and brakes on a test ride.

**Finance (hire purchase)** lets you start with more bikes for less cash, but monthly instalments are due whether bikes are rented or not. In some countries the lender keeps the original registration book until the loan is paid, which can matter for registration paperwork. Only finance as many bikes as your low-season income can cover.

## Registration, permits and insurance

Every country is different, but the same building blocks come up almost everywhere:

1. **A registered business.** A sole trader registration or a company, with a tax ID. Foreign owners often face ownership limits and need local advice.
2. **Valid registration and road tax for every bike,** renewed on time, with the plate and tax sticker displayed.
3. **Compulsory motor insurance.** This usually covers injury to people, not damage to the bike. Voluntary cover for theft or damage costs extra.
4. **Rules for renters.** Most countries require the rider to hold a licence valid for that class of bike, often a home licence plus an International Driving Permit for tourists.
5. **Any rental-specific permit.** Some places license rental vehicles separately, especially cars. Ask your local transport office whether this applies to motorbikes where you are.

## Pricing by day, week and month

Start with three prices per model: daily, weekly and monthly. Longer rentals get a lower daily rate because you save on handovers, cleaning and empty days.

To set them:

1. **Check the market.** Note what five nearby shops charge for the same model, and read their reviews to see what customers complain about.
2. **Work out your cost per bike per day.** Add up purchase cost spread over its working life, maintenance, insurance, tax, parking and your share of fixed costs, then divide by days in use.
3. **Set the daily rate at or slightly above the market** if your bikes are newer or your service is better. Do not start a price war.
4. **Discount for length,** but check each rate still covers your costs.

**Worked example (illustrative numbers only).** A shop renting a 125cc automatic:

| Rental length | Price | Works out per day | Discount vs daily |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 day | ฿250 | ฿250 | — |
| 1 week | ฿1,400 | ฿200 | 20% |
| 1 month | ฿3,500 | ฿117 | 53% |

Monthly renters pay less than half the daily rate, but a bike rented for 30 days in a row with one handover often earns more over a season than a bike waiting for walk-ins. Most shops adjust daily prices up in high season and keep monthly prices steadier.

## Deposits

A deposit gives the renter a reason to bring the bike back on time and in the same condition. Public listings show typical scooter deposits of around ฿3,000 for a 125cc bike in Phuket, rising to ฿10,000–15,000 for larger scooters ([Phuket 101](https://www.phuket101.net/renting-a-motorbike-in-phuket/)). Set yours by bike value and keep it simple.

Avoid holding passports. It is common, but it is restricted in several countries and creates problems if the renter needs the passport for a hotel, a bank or immigration. A cash, transfer or card deposit, plus a signed agreement and handover photos, protects you better. Our guide on [how much deposit to charge](/guides/rental-deposit-policy) covers sizes, payment methods and refunds in detail.

## Contracts and handover photos

Every rental needs a written agreement, even for one day. It should state:

- Renter's name, contact number, ID or passport number and licence details.
- Bike model, plate number, start and end date and time, and the price.
- Deposit amount and the conditions for deductions.
- Who pays for damage, theft, fines and towing.
- Where the bike may be ridden (for example, not off the island or across borders).
- A damage price list for common parts, attached or referenced.

Use our free [rental agreement](/resources/rental-agreement), which is bilingual and filled in with your details.

Then photograph the bike at every handover and return, in front of the renter, from the same angles every time. ## Helmets and safety kit

Helmets are not optional. In Thailand, for example, both rider and passenger must wear one and fines have recently increased. Beyond the law, a renter injured without a helmet is a disaster for them and for your reputation.

For each bike, keep:

- **Two helmets in at least three sizes across the fleet** (M, L, XL), certified to a recognised standard, with working buckles. Replace any helmet that has been dropped hard or involved in a crash.
- **Disposable hairnets or helmet liners** and a cleaning spray. Renters notice smelly helmets in reviews.
- **A phone holder,** which reduces renters riding one-handed while using maps.
- **A rain poncho** in the seat box in wet season.
- **A card with your number** and what to do after an accident or breakdown.

## Maintenance schedule

Rental bikes work harder than private bikes and are ridden by people who do not know them. Follow the owner's manual as the minimum, and keep a simple log per bike.

| When | What to check or do |
|---|---|
| Every handover | Tyres (pressure, cuts, tread), brakes, lights, horn, mirrors, fuel, any new damage |
| Every week | Chain or belt condition, battery charge, oil level, clean the bike |
| Every oil change interval (per manual; many shops do it sooner for rentals) | Engine oil, check air filter, spark plug, gear oil on schedule |
| Every 3 months | Brake pads, drive belt and rollers (automatics), cables, suspension |
| Every year | Road tax and compulsory insurance renewal, full service, safety inspection if required |

A bike that breaks down on a renter costs you more than the repair: a rescue trip, a replacement bike, a refund and possibly a bad review. Preventive maintenance is cheaper.

## Getting bookings

Most new shops get their first customers from a handful of channels. Do these well before spending on ads.

- **Google Business Profile.** Create a [Google Business Profile](https://business.google.com) with accurate opening hours, your location pin, photos of real bikes and prices. Ask happy customers for reviews and reply to every review, good or bad.
- **Social media.** Post your current available bikes, monthly deals and short videos. Facebook groups for expats and digital nomads in your area are a strong source of long-stay renters.
- **Chat apps.** Most renters will message you on WhatsApp, LINE, Telegram or Zalo, depending on the country. Reply fast and keep standard answers ready. Our [message templates](/resources/message-templates) cover prices, deposits, handover and return.
- **A booking page.** A simple page showing your bikes, prices and a booking form lets people book at midnight instead of waiting for you to answer. Lokalfleet includes a free booking page connected to your calendar, so you do not double-book.
- **Lokalkeys.** List your shop on [Lokalkeys](https://lokalkeys.com), where renters look specifically for independent local operators rather than big brands.
- **Partners.** Guesthouses, villa managers and co-working spaces will often recommend a reliable shop, especially if you deliver.

## Running costs and break-even

Before you buy anything, model your monthly costs and how long it takes to earn back your investment.

**Worked example (illustrative numbers only).** Ten used 125cc scooters bought at ฿35,000 each, a total of ฿350,000. Six are on monthly rentals at ฿3,500 and four rent daily at ฿250 with 60% of days booked.

| Monthly income | Amount |
|---|---|
| 6 bikes × ฿3,500 monthly | ฿21,000 |
| 4 bikes × 30 days × 60% × ฿250 | ฿18,000 |
| **Total income** | **฿39,000** |

| Monthly costs | With a shop | Delivery only, owner-run |
|---|---|---|
| Shop or yard rent | ฿10,000 | ฿0 |
| Part-time staff | ฿8,000 | ฿0 |
| Maintenance and parts (฿300 per bike) | ฿3,000 | ฿3,000 |
| Insurance, tax, registration (spread monthly) | ฿550 | ฿550 |
| Phone, utilities, fuel for delivery | ฿1,500 | ฿2,500 |
| Marketing and software | ฿1,000 | ฿1,000 |
| Damage and theft reserve (5% of income) | ฿1,950 | ฿1,950 |
| **Total costs** | **฿26,000** | **฿9,000** |
| **Monthly profit before your own pay** | **฿13,000** | **฿30,000** |
| **Months to earn back ฿350,000** | **about 27** | **about 12** |

Two lessons from this example. First, fixed costs like rent and staff decide whether a small fleet works, so many operators start from home or a small yard with delivery, then open a shop once the fleet is large enough. Second, a bike's resale value at the end matters: a well-kept bike you sell after three years returns part of your investment.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Buying too many bikes at once.** Start with what you can keep rented in low season.
- **Mixing too many models.** Every extra model means different parts, keys and know-how.
- **Holding passports instead of a proper deposit.** It causes arguments and, in several countries, legal trouble.
- **No written agreement or photos.** Without them, a damage dispute becomes your word against the renter's.
- **Skipping licence checks.** If a renter without a valid licence crashes, insurance may not pay and you may share the blame.
- **Letting insurance or tax lapse.** Put every renewal date in a calendar, per bike.
- **Ignoring reviews.** One unanswered complaint about a deposit can cost you many bookings.
- **Underpricing to win customers.** It is very hard to raise prices later, and your margin is your repair budget.
- **Keeping everything in your head or a notebook.** Once you have more than a few bikes, you need one place for bookings, deposits, photos and renewal dates.

## Your first 30 days

A simple order of work for the first month, once the bikes are bought and registered:

1. **Week 1: paperwork and kit.** Agreement printed or digital, damage price list, deposit rules, helmets, phone holders, a first-aid kit and a toolkit in the shop.
2. **Week 1: listings.** Google Business Profile live with real photos, a booking page, a Lokalkeys listing and your chat app number on everything.
3. **Week 2: partners.** Visit ten nearby guesthouses, villa managers and co-working spaces with a price card. Offer free delivery for their guests.
4. **Week 2–3: routine.** Practise the handover photos until they take under five minutes. Keep a maintenance log from the first day.
5. **Week 4: review.** Which bikes rented most? Which messages took longest to answer? Which questions came up again and again? Adjust prices, write standard replies, and decide whether you need more of one model.

After the first month, look at your numbers once a week: bikes rented, days empty, money taken, deposits held and repairs paid. Those five figures tell you more than any forecast.
