If you rent cars, scooters or motorbikes to consumers anywhere in the EU, a few pieces of EU law shape what your agreement can say and how you take money. They apply across the member states, with national laws on top. This guide covers the points that come up most for small rental businesses and links each one to the official text.

> This guide is general information, not legal advice. EU directives are applied through national law, and national rules add to them. Sources were checked on 8 November 2026. Have your agreement reviewed against the law of the country where you operate.

## Cancellation: no automatic 14-day right

The Consumer Rights Directive gives consumers 14 days to withdraw from most distance contracts, but Article 16(l) exempts car rental services where the contract sets a specific date or period ([Directive 2011/83/EU](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2011/83/oj/eng), accessed 8 November 2026). The EU's citizen portal tells travelers the same thing: for car hire booked online, by phone or by email, there's no automatic right to cancel, though the company's own terms may allow it ([Your Europe: hiring a car](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/driving-abroad/car-rental-abroad/index_en.htm), accessed 8 November 2026).

That makes your own cancellation terms important. Say how much notice gets a full refund, what you keep for late cancellations and no-shows, and how refunds are paid. Show them before the renter pays, not only in the signed agreement.

## Unfair terms

The Unfair Contract Terms Directive says a standard term that hasn't been individually negotiated is unfair if, against the requirement of good faith, it causes a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations to the consumer's detriment, and an unfair term does not bind the consumer ([Directive 93/13/EEC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/1993/13/oj/eng), accessed 8 November 2026). Terms must also be in plain, intelligible language.

In practice, clauses that tend to cause trouble in rental agreements are:

- Damage charges with no link to the real cost, or charged without evidence.
- Keeping the whole deposit for any breach, however small.
- Fuel policies that charge for a full tank the renter can't use.
- Fees that appear only in the small print.

In 2017, the European Commission and national consumer authorities secured commitments from five large rental groups on clearer prices, clearer insurance and waiver terms, clearer fuel options and fairer handling of alleged damage ([European Commission: coordinated actions on travel services](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/consumers/consumer-rights-and-complaints/enforcement-consumer-protection/coordinated-actions/other-travel-services_en), accessed 8 November 2026). Those are the same areas authorities look at for small businesses.

## Price, residence and insurance

Two more points from the EU's guidance to travelers ([Your Europe: hiring a car](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/driving-abroad/car-rental-abroad/index_en.htm), accessed 8 November 2026):

- **No price discrimination by nationality or residence.** EU rules ban treating customers differently because of their nationality or where they live. Charge the same price for the same rental whoever books it.
- **Third-party liability insurance is included.** Third-party liability cover valid in all EU countries must be included in the rental price. Optional extras (damage, theft, personal accident) are sold on top, and the agreement should say what each covers.

If renters may cross borders, ask them to tell you when they book, and make sure the vehicle meets the rules of the countries they'll visit, such as winter tires where those are compulsory.

## Deposits on cards: blocks need consent to the amount

A card deposit in the EU is usually a block (pre-authorization), not a charge. The Payment Services Directive covers this directly. Article 75 says that when a card payment's exact amount isn't known when the payer consents, the payer's provider may block funds only if the payer consented to the exact amount blocked, and must release the funds without undue delay once the exact amount is known, at the latest immediately after receiving the payment order. Recital 75 names car rental as an example ([Directive (EU) 2015/2366](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2015/2366/oj/eng), accessed 8 November 2026).

For your agreement and booking flow, that means:

- State the deposit amount, how it's held and when it's released.
- Get the renter's agreement to that exact amount before you block it.
- Release it promptly after return, and show deductions with evidence (handover photos, receipts).

Card blocks typically expire after a set number of days, depending on the card scheme and your provider, so they suit short rentals better than monthly ones. For long rentals, a deposit paid by transfer and held on record is often simpler. Our [deposit guide](/guides/rental-deposit-policy) compares the methods.

## Surcharges for card payments

Article 62(4) of the same directive bans charging consumers extra for payment instruments whose interchange fees are capped under EU rules, which covers most consumer debit and credit cards ([Directive (EU) 2015/2366](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2015/2366/oj/eng), accessed 8 November 2026). Build card costs into your prices instead of adding a card fee at checkout.

## GDPR: ID, license and passport copies

Renting vehicles means collecting personal data: name, address, ID or passport number, driving license details, often photos of the documents. The General Data Protection Regulation applies to all of it ([Regulation (EU) 2016/679](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng), accessed 8 November 2026). The principles that matter most here:

- **Lawful basis (Article 6).** Checking identity and license is part of performing the rental contract; keeping records for tax law is a legal obligation. Marketing needs its own basis, usually consent.
- **Data minimization (Article 5(1)(c)).** Collect what you need. If recording the license number, expiry and categories is enough for your insurer, you may not need to keep a full copy of a passport.
- **Storage limitation (Article 5(1)(e)).** Decide how long you keep documents after a rental ends (for example, until damage and fine claims can no longer arise) and delete them after that.
- **Transparency (Article 13).** Tell renters what you collect, why, how long you keep it and who you share it with, in a privacy notice linked from your booking page and agreement.
- **Security (Article 32).** Keep document photos out of personal phones' camera rolls and chat apps. Store them where access is controlled.

Some countries are stricter about copying ID. The Dutch government says only a few kinds of organizations, such as government bodies, banks and employers, may copy an ID, and they must say which legal obligation requires it ([Government of the Netherlands](https://www.government.nl/topics/identity-fraud/question-and-answer/which-organisations-are-allowed-to-make-a-copy-of-my-id), accessed 8 November 2026). Check your country's data protection authority before you make copies standard practice.

## A checklist for your EU agreement

- Business name, address, registration and VAT number; renter's name, contact and license details.
- Vehicle, plate, start and end dates and times, handover and return place.
- Total price including mandatory fees, and what's optional.
- Cancellation and no-show terms.
- Deposit amount, method, and release timing.
- Insurance included (third-party liability) and optional cover, with deductibles.
- Fuel or charge policy, mileage limits, cross-border rules.
- Who pays tolls, fines and towing, and how you pass them on.
- Damage process: inspection, photos, how costs are worked out.
- Privacy notice and how long you keep documents.
