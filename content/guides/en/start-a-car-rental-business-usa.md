A small car rental business in the US can start with three or four cars, a business registration and the right insurance. What makes it hard is that most of the rules sit at state and city level, so the paperwork in Texas looks different from Florida or California. This guide walks through the steps in the order you'll meet them and points you to the official sources.

> This guide is general information, not legal or tax advice. Rules differ by state, county and city and change over time. Sources were checked on 8 November 2026; confirm the details with your state and local offices before you start.

## Pick your customers and your model

Decide first who you rent to, because it shapes your fleet, your insurance and your prices.

- **Travelers and visitors** rent for a few days near airports, beaches, national parks and cities. They book late, compare prices online and care about a quick handover.
- **Locals between cars** (repairs, insurance replacement, new arrivals) rent for one to four weeks and often come through body shops and dealers.
- **Gig and delivery drivers** rent by the week or month. Demand is steady, but wear is high and you need clear rules on mileage and commercial use, which many insurance policies exclude unless you arrange it.
- **Monthly renters** (contractors, students, people waiting for a new car) rent one month at a time. See our [monthly car rental business guide](/guides/monthly-car-rental-business) for how that model works.

Many small operators also list on peer-to-peer platforms. If you run your own business alongside them, keep your own agreement, insurance and records, so the business doesn't depend on one platform's rules.

## Register the business

Every state lets you trade as a sole proprietor, but most rental operators form an LLC or corporation to separate business and personal assets. The US Small Business Administration explains the options and how licensing works at federal, state and local level, and suggests starting with your Secretary of State's website ([SBA: apply for licenses and permits](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/apply-licenses-permits), accessed 8 November 2026). SBA also notes that requirements and fees often vary by state.

You'll usually need:

1. **A state business registration** (LLC or corporation filing, or a trade name for a sole proprietor).
2. **An Employer Identification Number (EIN)** if you have employees, operate as a partnership or corporation, or pay certain taxes. The IRS issues EINs free online and warns against sites that charge for it ([IRS: get an EIN](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number), accessed 8 November 2026).
3. **Sales tax and rental tax registration** with your state revenue department (see the next section).
4. **A city or county business license** and, where you run a lot or office, zoning approval for parking rental cars there.
5. **A business bank account,** so rental income, deposits and costs stay separate from personal money.

## Rental taxes and surcharges

Car rentals are often taxed differently from other sales. Two examples show how much states vary:

| State | What applies | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Texas | Motor vehicle gross rental tax: 10% of gross receipts for rentals of 1 to 30 days, 6.25% for rentals over 30 days and up to 180 days. The rental company collects it from the customer. | [Texas Comptroller](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/motor-vehicle/gross-rental.php), accessed 8 November 2026 |
| Florida | A rental car surcharge of $2 per day, with a lower rate for some car-sharing use. | [Florida Department of Revenue, TIP 14A01-10](https://floridarevenue.com/taxes/tips/documents/TIP_14A01-10.pdf), accessed 8 November 2026 |

Many states, counties and airports add their own taxes or fees, and some cut the rate for longer rentals, as Texas does. Ask your state revenue department which taxes apply, how to show them on the invoice and how often to file. Set this up before your first rental, because you collect the tax from renters as you go.

## Insurance

Insurance is the biggest running cost after the cars themselves, and the area where getting it wrong costs most.

**Minimum liability.** Every state sets minimum liability limits for vehicles on its roads. California, for example, raised its minimums on 1 January 2025 to $30,000 per person and $60,000 per accident for injury or death, and $15,000 for property damage ([California Department of Insurance](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/0400-news/0102-alerts/2025/New-Year-Means-New-Changes-for-Insurance.cfm), accessed 8 November 2026). Your policy has to meet the minimums of the state where the cars are registered, and many operators buy well above them.

**A commercial policy written for rental.** A personal auto policy does not cover renting cars to the public. Ask a commercial insurance agent for cover that names rental use, and check who counts as a covered driver, whether there's a minimum renter age, and whether delivery or rideshare use is excluded.

**Physical damage.** Liability covers harm to others, not damage to your own car. Decide whether to insure your cars for collision and theft, or to carry that risk yourself with a deposit and a damage policy. Our [rental insurance guide for the US and UK](/guides/car-rental-insurance-us-uk) covers the options, including damage waivers and how some states regulate them.

**Vicarious liability.** Under 49 U.S.C. 30106, often called the Graves Amendment, a business that rents or leases vehicles isn't liable under state law just because it owns the vehicle, as long as it wasn't negligent and didn't commit a crime ([49 U.S.C. 30106, govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/49/30106), accessed 8 November 2026). It does not protect you from your own negligence, so checking licenses and maintaining the cars still matter.

## Safety recalls

Federal law gives the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration recall authority over rental companies and stops them renting out vehicles with an open safety recall until it's fixed. The rule took effect on 1 June 2016. It applies to a "rental company", defined as a business that rents vehicles and uses a fleet of 35 or more rental vehicles, on average, during the calendar year ([49 U.S.C. chapter 301, section 30102](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2023-title49/html/USCODE-2023-title49-subtitleVI-partA-chap301.htm), accessed 8 November 2026).

If your fleet is smaller, it's still worth checking each car's VIN on [NHTSA's recall lookup](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls) when you buy it and every few months. A crash caused by a known, unrepaired defect is exactly the kind of negligence that insurance and the Graves Amendment may not cover.

## Choosing cars

Start with cars that are cheap to run, easy to fix and easy to resell:

- **Compact and midsize sedans** (Corolla, Civic, Elantra class) rent well, cost least to insure and hold value.
- **Small SUVs** earn a higher daily rate in tourist and mountain areas.
- **Minivans and 7-seaters** suit families and groups but have fewer customers.

Buy two or three of the same model where you can, so parts, tires and know-how carry across. Check each car's title, history report and open recalls before you buy, and budget for tires and brakes in the first year.

## Pricing

Set daily, weekly and monthly rates for each car. Look at what local agencies and peer-to-peer hosts charge for the same class, then work out your own cost per car per day: payment or depreciation, insurance, registration, maintenance, cleaning, parking, taxes you can't pass on, and a reserve for damage and empty days.

Longer rentals get a lower daily rate because you save on handovers and cleaning. Our free [pricing calculator](/resources/pricing-calculator) works out the minimum daily, weekly and monthly price that still covers your costs at the utilization you expect.

## Rental agreement, deposits and license checks

Every rental needs a signed agreement, even for a day. It should cover the renter and any additional drivers, the car and its condition, the dates and price, mileage limits, fuel, the deposit, who pays for damage, tolls and tickets, and where the car may be driven. Our guide on [what to put in a rental agreement](/guides/rental-agreement-what-to-include) goes clause by clause, and you can start from the free [rental agreement template](/resources/rental-agreement).

Check every driver's license against the person in front of you and record the details. Take a deposit sized to your insurance deductible, and hold it the same way every time (see [how much deposit to charge](/guides/rental-deposit-policy)). Photograph the car at every handover and return from the same angles, using a [handover checklist](/guides/vehicle-handover-checklist).

## Getting your first bookings

- **Google Business Profile** with your real lot address, hours, photos of the actual cars and prices.
- **Your own booking page,** so people can check availability and book at night without calling.
- **Local partners:** body shops, dealers, hotels and short-term rental hosts often refer renters to a reliable local business.
- **Peer-to-peer platforms** can fill gaps while you build direct bookings.
