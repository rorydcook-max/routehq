A rental agreement is the one document that protects you when something goes wrong: a scratch nobody remembers, a speeding ticket that arrives a month later, a scooter returned two days late. Most disputes in small rental businesses are not about bad faith. They happen because the owner and the renter remember the deal differently.

This guide walks through what a good vehicle rental agreement covers, clause by clause, for small car and scooter rental businesses in South-East Asia and beyond. At the end there's a free template you can download and adapt.

> Rules on rental contracts, deposits, insurance and consumer rights differ by country, and sometimes by province or city. Treat this guide as a checklist, not legal advice, and have a local lawyer review your agreement before you use it.

## The parties and the vehicle

Start with who is renting what. Write the full legal name of your business (or your own name if you trade as an individual), your address and how to reach you. For the renter, record their full name as it appears on their passport or ID, their nationality, a phone number they will answer during the rental, and where they are staying.

Then describe the vehicle precisely: make, model, year, colour, registration (plate) number and, if you track it, the chassis or engine number. Add the odometer reading and fuel level at handover. If a helmet, child seat, phone holder or second key goes with the vehicle, list it, so a missing item is never a matter of opinion.

## Rental period and return

State the start date and time, the end date and time, and exactly where the vehicle will be handed over and returned. If you deliver to a hotel or villa, say so. Explain how an extension works: for example, that the renter must ask before the end time and that an extension only counts once you confirm it in writing (a message is fine).

## Price, payment and deposit

Write the rental rate and the billing period (per day, per week, per month), the total for the agreed period, what is included, and when and how payment is due. If you charge for delivery, a second driver or extras, list those prices too.

The deposit deserves its own clause. Say how much it is, how it is paid (cash, transfer, card hold), what it can be used for (unpaid rent, damage, fuel, cleaning, fines, late return, missing items) and when it is returned: for example, within a set number of days after the return inspection, once you have checked there are no outstanding fines. Being clear here prevents the most common argument in the business.

## Damage, excess and how damage is assessed

Agree in advance how damage will be judged. The fairest method is photos or a short video of the whole vehicle at handover and again at return, taken together with the renter, with existing scratches and dents noted on a condition sheet that both of you sign.

Then explain what happens if there is new damage:

- how repair costs are worked out (a quote from a named garage, or a published price list for common parts like mirrors and panels on scooters),
- the maximum the renter pays per incident if you offer insurance with an excess (sometimes called a deductible), and
- whether loss of rental income while the vehicle is being repaired can be charged.

## Fuel and mileage

Say what fuel level the vehicle goes out with and how it must come back. A common rule is "same level as at pickup", with a refuelling charge if it comes back lower. If there is a daily or total kilometre limit, state it, along with the charge per extra kilometre. If there is no limit, write "unlimited" so nobody has to wonder.

## Who may drive, and licences

List every person allowed to drive, with their licence number, issuing country and expiry date. Require a licence valid for that class of vehicle: a car licence does not always cover a motorbike, and many countries expect foreign drivers to carry an International Driving Permit alongside their home licence. Make the renter responsible for anyone they let drive who is not on the agreement.

## Insurance and what it doesn't cover

Describe the insurance on the vehicle in plain words: what type it is, roughly what it covers, and the excess. Just as important is what it does **not** cover. Typical exclusions include driving without a valid licence, driving under the influence of alcohol or drugs, off-road use, and unauthorised drivers. Tell renters about these exclusions before they sign, because these are the cases where they may have to pay for everything themselves.

## Accidents, breakdowns and police reports

Set out what the renter must do after an accident: make sure everyone is safe, call you straight away, not admit liability or agree a private settlement without you, take photos, and get a police report where one is needed for the insurance claim. For breakdowns, say who to call and that the renter must not authorise repairs without your approval, except to keep people safe.

## Traffic fines and tolls

Make the renter responsible for traffic and parking fines, tolls and impound costs during the rental, even when the fine arrives after the vehicle is returned. Say how you will pass these on, for example by deducting them from the deposit with a copy of the fine, or invoicing them, and whether you add a handling fee.

## Late return and cancellation

Explain what happens if the vehicle comes back late: a grace period, then an hourly or daily charge. Say when a vehicle that has not been returned or extended will be treated as overdue, and that you may recover it.

For cancellations, state how much notice the renter must give for a full or partial refund of any prepayment, and what happens if you have to cancel (for example, offering a similar vehicle or a full refund).

## Prohibited uses

List what the vehicle must not be used for: racing, towing, carrying more passengers than it is built for, commercial use such as ride-hailing or deliveries (unless agreed), sub-letting it to someone else, taking it off-road, or taking it outside an agreed area, such as onto a ferry or across a border, without permission.

## Governing law and disputes

Say which country's law applies to the agreement and where any dispute would be heard. It's also worth adding that both sides will first try to resolve a disagreement by talking it through. Your lawyer can tell you the right wording for where you operate.

## Signatures and ID copies

Finish with the date, the names and signatures of both parties, and a note of the ID documents you have seen or copied (passport, licence, International Driving Permit). Keep a copy of the signed agreement, the condition photos and the ID copies together for each rental, for as long as fines or claims could still arrive.

## Make it easy to sign

A clear two- or three-page agreement that a renter can read in five minutes is worth more than a long one nobody reads. Use short clauses and plain words, put the money (rate, deposit, excess, late fees) where it is easy to find, and offer it in the renter's language where you can. A digital agreement signed on a phone, with photos attached, saves paper and is much harder to lose.
